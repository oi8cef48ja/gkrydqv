Understanding Peripheral Neuropathy: When Numbness Becomes Dangerous

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Peripheral neuropathy occurs when nerves outside the brain and spinal cord become damaged, causing gradual numbness, tingling, burning pain, or muscle weakness. In people with diabetes, symptoms often start in the feet and can lead to serious cuts, ulcers, or infections when sensation is lost.

Key warning signs include persistent tingling, sharp or burning pain, loss of balance, and reduced ability to feel temperature or injury. Effective management strategies include tight blood sugar control, daily foot inspections, proper footwear, and regular monitoring by a healthcare provider.

Because peripheral neuropathy can progress silently and lead to irreversible nerve damage, catching symptoms early is critical. Explanation

Peripheral neuropathy occurs when the peripheral nerves—those outside the brain and spinal cord—are damaged. These nerves carry signals for sensation, movement and organ functions. When they malfunction, you might notice unusual sensations, weakness or a complete loss of feeling. In people with diabetes, this nerve damage often leads to loss of feeling in feet, making even minor injuries a serious threat.

What Is Peripheral Neuropathy?

Peripheral neuropathy can affect one nerve (mononeuropathy) or many nerves (polyneuropathy). The most common form in diabetes is distal symmetric polyneuropathy, which usually starts in the toes and gradually moves up the legs and hands.

Key features:

Gradual onset of numbness or tingling

"Pins and needles" sensations

Burning or sharp pain

Muscle weakness or cramping

Loss of balance

Why "Loss of Feeling in Feet" Matters in Diabetes

High blood sugar over time damages small blood vessels that nourish nerves, especially in the hands and feet. When you lose sensory feedback in your feet, you can't feel:

Cuts, scrapes or blisters

Pressure from ill-fitting shoes

Extreme temperatures (hot pavement, scalding water)

Without pain as a warning system, small injuries can worsen into infections, ulcers or bone damage. In severe cases, untreated foot injuries may lead to amputation.

Signs That Numbness Is Becoming Dangerous

Not all numbness requires emergency care—but some red flags demand prompt attention:

Sudden or rapidly spreading numbness

Severe, unrelenting pain despite over-the-counter medications

Weakness in one leg or arm affecting walking or grip

Loss of bladder or bowel control

High fever with new numbness (possible infection)

Open sores, ulcers or red/swollen areas on the feet

Managing and Treating Peripheral Neuropathy

While nerve damage can't always be reversed, early intervention and consistent self-care can slow progression and reduce complications.

Blood Sugar Control

Aim for target A1C levels as recommended by your doctor

Check blood glucose regularly

Follow a balanced diet and exercise plan

Foot Care

Inspect your feet daily for cuts, blisters or redness

Wear well-fitting, supportive shoes and clean socks

Keep feet clean and dry; moisturize to prevent cracks

Trim toenails straight across; avoid self-treatment of calluses

Medications and Supplements

Prescription pain relievers (e.g., certain antidepressants or anticonvulsants)

Topical treatments (capsaicin cream, lidocaine patches)

B-vitamin supplements if deficiency is present

Physical Therapy and Exercise

Strengthening and balance exercises to reduce fall risk

Low-impact activities (walking, swimming, cycling)

Lifestyle Modifications

Quit smoking; avoid excessive alcohol

Maintain a healthy weight

Manage stress to improve overall nerve health

Monitoring for Complications

Regular checkups are crucial. Schedule visits with your healthcare team at least twice a year, or more often if:

Your blood sugar is difficult to control

You have chronic wounds or foot ulcers
