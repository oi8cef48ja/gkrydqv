Explanation

Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining that can cause discomfort, indigestion and, in serious cases, bleeding. While gastritis isn't always life-threatening, it can affect your quality of life if left untreated. Below, we'll explore the five most common causes of gastritis that doctors encounter and practical steps you can take to heal your stomach lining.

What Is Gastritis?

Gastritis occurs when the protective mucus layer of your stomach is weakened, allowing acid and digestive enzymes to irritate or damage the tissue beneath. Common symptoms include:

Upper abdominal pain or burning

Nausea or vomiting

Bloating and belching

Loss of appetite

Occasional bloody vomit or dark stools (more serious)

Symptoms can range from mild discomfort to severe pain. If you experience any alarming signs (for example, black stools or vomiting blood), speak to a doctor immediately.

5 Common Causes of Gastritis

Doctors often identify one or more of these triggers when diagnosing gastritis:

1. Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) Infection

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen, aspirin and naproxen can irritate the stomach lining over time.

3. Excessive Alcohol Consumption

Drinking heavily or frequently can erode the stomach's mucus barrier.

Alcohol increases acid production and slows healing

Binge drinking causes acute (sudden) gastritis, leading to sharp pain and vomiting

Chronic alcohol use can lead to long-term inflammation and complications

4. Stress-Related Gastritis

Severe physical stress—such as from surgery, injury, burns or serious infections—can lead to "stress ulcers."

The body's stress response diverts blood away from the stomach lining

Reduced blood flow weakens protective mechanisms

Stress ulcers can bleed; they often require hospitalization and close monitoring

5. Autoimmune and Other Diseases

In some cases, the immune system mistakenly attacks stomach cells (autoimmune gastritis). Other related conditions include:

Pernicious anemia (linked to vitamin B12 deficiency)

Crohn's disease or other inflammatory bowel diseases

Viral infections (e.g., cytomegalovirus in immunocompromised people)

Radiation or chemo treatments

How to Heal Gastritis: Practical Strategies

Healing gastritis involves removing or treating the underlying cause, relieving symptoms and supporting the stomach lining as it recovers. Here's a step-by-step approach:

1. Get a Proper Diagnosis

Visit your doctor for tests (blood work, breath test or endoscopy) if symptoms persist or worsen.

2. Medications to Reduce Acid

Proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs) such as omeprazole or esomeprazole lower stomach acid production.

H2 blockers (e.g., ranitidine or famotidine) reduce acid secretion.

Antacids provide quick relief by neutralizing existing acid.

3. Antibiotics for H. pylori

If an H. pylori infection is confirmed, a typical treatment plan includes:

Two antibiotics (such as clarithromycin plus amoxicillin or metronidazole)

A PPI to protect and heal the stomach lining

Treatment usually lasts 10–14 days; follow your prescription exactly

4. Lifestyle and Dietary Changes

Making adjustments to daily habits can support healing and prevent recurrence:

• Avoid alcohol, spicy foods, caffeine and acidic drinks (e.g., citrus juices) • Eat smaller, more frequent meals rather than large ones • Quit smoking—tobacco worsens inflammation and delays healing • Manage stress with relaxation techniques (yoga, meditation, gentle exercise) • Limit or switch from NSAIDs; discuss safer alternatives with your doctor

5. Supportive Supplements and Foods

Certain nutrients and foods may help soothe the stomach lining:

• Probiotics (yogurt or supplements) to balance gut bacteria • Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) to form a protective barrier • L-glutamine for tissue repair (check with your doctor before starting) • High-fiber foods (whole grains, vegetables) to promote healthy digestion

When to Seek Medical Attention

Most cases of mild gastritis improve with home care and medication. However, be vigilant and contact a healthcare professional if you experience:

Severe or persistent abdominal pain

Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds

Black, tarry stools

Difficulty swallowing or unexplained weight loss

Signs of anemia (fatigue, shortness of breath, pale skin)

Talking to Your Doctor

Always keep an open line of communication with your healthcare provider. Share details about:

Any over-the-counter or prescription medications you take regularly

Your alcohol and smoking habits

Recent illnesses, surgeries or stressful events

Dietary patterns and any food intolerances

Your doctor can tailor treatment to your specific situation, adjust medications, and monitor your progress.
