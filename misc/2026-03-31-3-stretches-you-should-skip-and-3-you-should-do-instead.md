Alternative Recommendation: The Cross-Body Stretch

"Take your arm, and you're going to pull across your body," says Dr. Horschig. Hold for 10 to 30 seconds, bringing your arm back in front of you to reset. Pull across gently; don't pull across too hard. You can do the stretch standing up or lying down. Doing it lying down will help keep your shoulder blade in a tighter position instead of letting it wing away from the body.

2) Weighted Thoracic Spine Mobilization

"A lot of people need to improve their thoracic extension. We sit at computers all day; we look at our phones. We're very kyphotic in our upper back," says Dr. Horschig. "We're limited in how much motion we can extend that back. That's gonna hurt our ability to press overhead any back squats, front squats, and keep our chest upright."

This stretch uses a foam roller and a weight with arms overhead for more T-spine extension which can lead to an over extension from the lower back, and cause shoulder issues of its own, too.

"You may think you're stretching your mid-back, but notice the position of my low back. I'm extremely arched. That is not efficient," he says as he demonstrates.

What he recommends instead: Thoracic Spine Mobilization with Braced Core

Using the same foam roller, ditch the weight. "Brace your core... I don't want any movement from you low back. Hands up, and all we're doing is arching just a little bit, sort of teeter-tottering over with that mid-back. We can roll up and go to another segment."

You're not just foam-rolling in this case, either. You're stopping at several moments and actually moving with your back (which is key during all foam rolling, by the way: Pin tissues, then move around).

3) Pec Stretching with Shoulder Pressure

You've seen it, and maybe done it yourself. When you use some type of rig or wall to twist and stretch out your tight chest, you're not doing yourself any favors.

"You're often feeling this a ton in your shoulder joint, putting a lot of pressure on the front side (capsule) of your shoulder joint, and not efficiently stretching the pec," says Dr. Horschig.

What he recommends instead: Tack and Stretch with a Lacrosse Ball

This is a tactic done by a host of trainers, including Horschig. Much like the foam-rolling drill, you're aiming to use the lacrosse ball to "pin" your pectoral tissue into a position, then moving at the shoulder to loosen the tissue while in that pinned position.

"Take a ball and pin it against a wall (or rig) and place it we want to place it right in that pec," says Dr. Horschig. "Pin and move the arm up, flexing the arm up and down. It's an active release stretch of that pec muscle."

Active releases are generally better than pure stretches, especially when it comes to stretching tight pecs. They're safer and they target the chest tissues instead of just moving you through that shoulder capsule.
