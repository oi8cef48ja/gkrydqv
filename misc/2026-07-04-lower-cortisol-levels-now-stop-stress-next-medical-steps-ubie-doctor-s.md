Always Stressed? How to Lower Cortisol Levels & Medical Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

How to lower cortisol naturally: Get 7–9 hours of consistent sleep, practice daily breathing or mindfulness, do moderate exercise with proper recovery, eat balanced meals to stabilize blood sugar, nurture social connections, and reduce alcohol and nicotine. These evidence-based habits help regulate your body's stress response and support healthy cortisol levels.

Doctors may order blood, saliva, or 24-hour urine cortisol tests, plus thyroid and glucose panels, to guide treatment.

Explanation

If you feel "on edge" most of the time, wake up tired, or struggle to relax even when nothing is wrong, you may be wondering how to lower cortisol levels.

Cortisol is often called the "stress hormone." It's produced by your adrenal glands and plays an essential role in your body. In normal amounts, cortisol:

Helps regulate blood sugar

Controls inflammation

Supports metabolism

Maintains blood pressure

Helps you wake up and stay alert

Cortisol isn't the enemy. In fact, you need it to survive. The problem happens when stress becomes chronic and cortisol remains elevated for long periods.

Below is a clear, evidence-based guide on how to lower cortisol levels safely — and when it's time to speak to a doctor.

What Causes High Cortisol?

Short-term stress (a work deadline, illness, public speaking) temporarily raises cortisol. That's normal.

Chronic stress can keep cortisol elevated. Common causes include:

Ongoing work or financial stress

Relationship conflict

Poor sleep

Overtraining or excessive exercise

Chronic illness

Untreated anxiety or depression

Certain medications (like long-term steroid use)

In rare cases, high cortisol may be caused by a medical condition such as Cushing's syndrome or adrenal disorders. These require medical evaluation.

Signs Your Cortisol May Be Elevated

High cortisol does not always cause obvious symptoms. But over time, people may notice:

Persistent fatigue despite sleep

Trouble falling or staying asleep

Weight gain (especially around the abdomen)

Increased cravings for sugar or salty foods

Irritability or anxiety

Brain fog

High blood pressure

Frequent infections

These symptoms are common and can overlap with anxiety, depression, thyroid issues, and other health problems. That's why guessing is not helpful — proper evaluation matters.

How to Lower Cortisol Levels Naturally

The good news: most people can significantly reduce cortisol through lifestyle changes. These methods are supported by medical research and clinical practice.

1. Improve Sleep First

Sleep is one of the most powerful ways to lower cortisol levels.

When you don't sleep enough, cortisol rises the next day. Chronic sleep deprivation keeps stress hormones elevated.

To improve sleep:

Go to bed and wake up at the same time daily

Keep your bedroom dark and cool

Avoid screens 60 minutes before bed

Limit caffeine after noon

Avoid heavy meals late at night

Aim for 7–9 hours of quality sleep per night.

If insomnia persists for more than a few weeks, speak to a doctor.

2. Practice Daily Stress Regulation (Not Just Relaxation)

You don't need to eliminate stress. You need to help your nervous system recover from it.

Effective methods to lower cortisol levels include:

Slow breathing exercises (4–6 breaths per minute)

Meditation or mindfulness (10–20 minutes daily)

Gentle yoga

Spending time in nature

Journaling

Prayer or reflective practices

Even 10 minutes per day can reduce stress hormone output.

Consistency matters more than intensity.

3. Exercise — But Don't Overdo It

Exercise is one of the most reliable ways to regulate cortisol over time.

However, very intense or prolonged workouts without recovery can temporarily raise cortisol.

For most adults:

150 minutes per week of moderate exercise is ideal

Strength training 2–3 times per week

Walking daily

If you feel exhausted, wired, or unable to recover after workouts, you may need to scale back.

Balance is key.

4. Stabilize Blood Sugar

Blood sugar swings trigger cortisol release.

To help lower cortisol levels naturally:

Eat balanced meals with protein, fiber, and healthy fats

Avoid skipping meals

Reduce highly processed sugar

Limit excessive caffeine

Stay hydrated

Extreme dieting or prolonged fasting can increase cortisol in some people.

5. Reduce Chronic Mental Load

Chronic psychological stress is one of the biggest drivers of elevated cortisol.

You can't remove all stress, but you can reduce unnecessary strain:

Set boundaries at work

Limit exposure to constant negative news

Delegate tasks when possible

Seek therapy or counseling

Address unresolved trauma

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) has strong evidence for reducing anxiety and stress-related hormone imbalance.

6. Strengthen Social Connections

Humans regulate stress better in supportive environments.

Research shows that positive relationships help buffer cortisol spikes.

Prioritize:

Regular time with trusted friends or family

Honest conversations

Community or group activities

Isolation tends to worsen stress hormone imbalance.

7. Limit Alcohol and Nicotine

Alcohol may feel calming short-term, but it disrupts sleep and raises cortisol later.

Nicotine stimulates stress hormones directly.

Reducing or eliminating these substances often improves stress resilience.

Lifestyle changes help most people. But sometimes medical evaluation is necessary.

Persistent fatigue despite lifestyle improvements

Irregular menstrual cycles

Unusual hair growth or hair loss

A doctor may order:

Blood cortisol testing

Saliva cortisol testing

24-hour urine cortisol

Thyroid testing

Blood sugar tests

Do not self-diagnose adrenal problems based on internet advice. True endocrine disorders are uncommon but serious.

If you experience chest pain, severe shortness of breath, confusion, fainting, or suicidal thoughts, seek urgent medical care immediately.

If you're constantly stressed, it's not a personal failure.

Modern life often keeps the nervous system in a constant "alert" state.

The goal is not to eliminate cortisol — it's to restore rhythm and recovery.
