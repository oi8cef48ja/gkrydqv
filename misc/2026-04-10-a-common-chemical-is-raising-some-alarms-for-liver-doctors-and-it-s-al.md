# A Common Chemical is 'Raising Some Alarms' for Liver Doctors. And It's Already in Your House.

WHEN YOU THINK about the major causes of liver disease, alcohol or high cholesterol might come to mind. Fresh laundry and a clean house? Not so much.

Yet that's exactly what scientists are warning could harm your liver. More specifically, a chemical widely found in dry cleaners and cleaning products like spot removers and stainless steel adhesives.

The colorless, man-made toxin is called tetrachloroethylene (PCE). You may also know it from its other name, perchloroethylene. A study published in Liver International earlier this month warns exposure to PCE could triple the risk for severe liver scarring.

"The findings suggest that exposure to PCE may be the reason why one person develops liver disease while someone with the exact same health and demographic profile does not," explains Brian P. Lee, MD, MAS, hepatologist and liver transplant specialist with Keck Medicine of USC and lead author of the study.

Before you swear off cleaning forever, toxicologists say there are other ways to reduce your exposure. Plus, ways to protect your liver health going forward.

What Did the Study Find?

The study authors analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, zeroing in on blood samples from participants aged 20 and up that were collected between 2017 and 2020. About 7 percent of people had detectable levels of PCE in their blood.

Further analysis of the health data showed people with detectable levels of PCE were three times more likely to have significant liver fibrosis (excessive scar tissue) than those who weren’t exposed to the chemical. Every one nanogram per milliliter increase in PCE in the blood was linked to a five times greater risk of liver fibrosis.

Who is Most at Risk for PCE Exposure?

The researchers found some interesting patterns. For example, people with higher incomes were more likely to have detectable levels of PCE in their blood. According to the authors, this might be because this income bracket probably use dry-cleaning services more often.

But it’s also worth pointing out that PCE can be found in products such as adhesive glues, spot removers, and stainless steel polish. PCE has also been found in soil and groundwater. Basically, don’t assume this isn’t a potential issue for you if you don’t dry-clean your clothes.

How PCE Raises Liver Disease Risk

The study only found a link between PCE exposure and liver disease. It didn’t dive into why this exists. But there are a few theories on why PCE might be so harmful.

First, you need to look at how your body breaks down PCE, says Lee. PCE gets into the body through skin contact or breathing contaminated air. “PCE then travels to the liver, where it’s processed and broken down into byproducts that react with liver cells to cause liver inflammation and scarring,” he adds. Lee also warns that increased inflammation and scarring may lead to cirrhosis, which can then raise your liver cancer risk.

The chemical byproducts when PCE is broken down can injure mitochondria in liver cells as well, says Kelly Johnson-Arbor, MD, toxicologist at MedStar Health. When the powerhouse of your cell can't do its job, it can lead to more cell and tissue damage.

It's not just your liver health you need to worry about, either. “Tetrachloroethylene can also build up in various tissues, including the brain and kidneys, as well as fat,” Johnson-Arbor says. “Acute inhalation of tetrachloroethylene can cause eye irritation, coughing, dizziness, and headaches, while chronic exposure can cause liver and kidney disease.”

How Concerned Should You Be?

As you can imagine, having PCE in your body isn’t great. “This should be raising some alarms for people,” warns Jamie Alan, PhD, associate professor of pharmacology and toxicology at Michigan State University. “This is one of the first reports linking clinical complications with dose-dependent exposure.”

Previous research on PCE focused on people who regularly have close exposure to the chemical, like employees at dry-cleaning facilities. Lee says his study shows PCE exposure is a potential problem for everyone. “These results show that you can be exposed to PCE amounts that affect your health without even realizing it,” he says.

Still, Johnson-Arbor recommends keeping things in perspective. While 7 percent of people have detectable levels of PCE in their blood, that meant that 93 percent don’t. “That is reassuring and suggests that the vast majority of adults in the United States likely do not have significant exposures to this chemical,” she says.

Since the chemical slowly evaporates off of clothes that have been recently dry-cleaned, Johnson-Arbor suggests unwrapping your freshly-cleaned clothes and letting them air out a little before putting them in your closet.
